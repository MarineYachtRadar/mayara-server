Quantum networking explained

Every Raymarine Quantum is a WiFi radar inside, even the ones you connect with a cable. Once you know why, several odd things about them stop being odd.

The problem: getting a picture off something that spins

A radar antenna turns, typically 24 to 48 times a minute, for years on end. The electronics that generate and process the picture sit up there with it, turning too. Somehow the picture has to reach the fixed part of the radar, and from there your chart plotter.

The traditional answer is a slip ring: a stack of metal rings on the rotating shaft with brushes pressed against them, carrying power and data across the joint. It works, and it is a wear item — brushes wear down, contacts corrode in salt air, and a noisy contact puts noise straight into the signal.

Raymarine's answer: don't cross the joint at all

A Quantum sends its picture across the gap by 2.4 GHz WiFi. The rotating head has a radio, and the picture flies off it through the air. Only power goes through the joint, and power is the easy part — no signal integrity to worry about, and far more tolerant of a worn contact.

Where that picture lands depends on the model. On a wireless Quantum it goes straight to your chart plotter. On a cable-capable one it lands on a small bridge board a few centimetres below, in the radar's own base, which then pushes it out of the socket.

Two ways to get the picture out of the base

Once the picture has landed in the fixed base, it has to reach your chart plotter — and this is where the two Quantum variants differ. The wireless-only model simply keeps going over the air. The cable-capable models add a small bridge board in the base that joins the radar's own internal network and pushes the picture out of the socket.

Nearly every current Quantum is cable-capable

Which one you have is mostly a question of vintage. The WiFi-only radome — the Q24W — has been discontinued. The radomes sold since, the Quantum Q24C and the Quantum 2 Q24D, all carry a RayNet socket alongside their WiFi, and so do the Cyclone open arrays.

The practical consequence: on most installations today there is a bridge board sitting in the radar's base whether or not you use the cable. That is why the internal network described below turns up in WiFi scans on boats whose owners are certain their radar is "wired".

Why this is a neat piece of design

The wear item is gone. There is no data slip ring to corrode, and the rotating joint carries only power. It also means the wireless model is the simpler product, not the cut-down one — a wired Quantum is the same radar plus one extra board. And because the internal link is the same WiFi the outside world uses, the same radio does both jobs on wireless installations.

The two network names

Both are built from the radar's seven-digit serial number, and they do completely different jobs. Neither is something you normally need to touch.

NameWho puts it on the airWhen you see it
Quantum_1234567 Your chart plotter, while it is pairing It is the name printed on the radar's label, and it is the network the radar goes looking for. During pairing the chart plotter puts a network of exactly that name on the air so the radar will join it; once the radar is paired the plotter hands over its real network details and switches back to its own name. Raymarine also documents that a never-paired radar can be found by scanning for this name, if you have lost the label.
Quantum_W3_1234567 The bridge board in the radar's base — present on every cable-capable model, which is nearly all of them now It is the internal link between the spinning head and the base. It is not printed on the label, it is not part of setup, and it is normally present the whole time the radar is powered. The exact name can vary; this is the usual form.

So if you scan for WiFi networks near a Quantum and see a name with the radar's serial number in it, nothing is wrong — you are looking at the radar's plumbing, not at a network you are meant to join.

What this means for you

  • 2.4 GHz congestion can cost you picture quality even on a wired radar. Microwave ovens, Bluetooth, a crowded marina, or your own boat WiFi on a clashing channel all share that band. If a Quantum's picture goes patchy, moving your WiFi to a quieter channel is a real fix — and that advice applies just as much to a cabled installation, which surprises people.
  • It explains why a Quantum sends so little data. A radar whose only data path is a modest WiFi link on a spinning platform has to be frugal. A Quantum sends fewer and shorter spokes than any other radar Mayara supports, and compresses them on top of that. How much each radar sends.
  • A WiFi scan near a wired Quantum may still show a network named after the radar — typically Quantum_W3_1234567, with your radar's serial. That is the internal link described above. It is entirely normal, it is not something you need to join, and joining it will not hand you a radar picture on its own.
  • Siting matters more than for other radars. Height and a clear line of sight help the radar; on a wireless installation, the distance and obstructions between the radar and your chart plotter are part of the radar's data path, not just a convenience.
