Radar networking

How radars put data on the network, why the radar itself belongs on wired Ethernet, and what to check when nothing is detected.

How a radar reaches Mayara

Almost every network radar announces itself and sends its picture using UDP multicast (Koden and the Furuno DRS4W use UDP broadcast, which behaves the same way for our purposes). The radar sends one copy of each spoke to a group address; every interested listener on the same Ethernet segment receives it. No connection is set up, nothing is acknowledged, and nothing is ever retransmitted.

That design is why the radar's traffic cannot cross a subnet boundary, a VPN, or a mesh WiFi backhaul: multicast stays on the local segment unless a network administrator has deliberately configured it to be routed. Mayara and the radar must share a network segment.

"Same segment" is about switching, not about hardware. The LAN ports of a typical boat WiFi router are a built-in switch, so plugging both the radar and the Mayara machine into them is fine — the traffic never gets routed. What breaks it is the radar and Mayara ending up on different subnets or VLANs, the radar sitting behind the router's WAN port, or a tunnel of any kind between the two.

Why the radar itself must be wired

On wired Ethernet, multicast is delivered at full wire speed with no overhead. On WiFi it is a different story. Multicast frames have no single recipient, so the access point cannot use the fast data rate it negotiated with an individual client. Instead it must fall back to the base rate — the lowest mandatory rate of the standard — so that every associated client can still decode the frame.

A Navico radar produces roughly 1 MB/s (about 8 Mbit/s) of spoke data. At the 802.11b base rate of 1 Mbit/s that is simply impossible. Even at the 6 Mbit/s base rate of modern standards, the radar alone consumes most of the available multicast bandwidth before WiFi framing overhead — and since multicast is never retransmitted, everything that does not fit is lost for good.

What a WiFi radar path actually looks like

Missing spokes and wedge-shaped gaps in the picture, an image that freezes and jumps, and a WiFi network that becomes slow for everyone else on the same access point — because multicast at the base rate eats airtime out of all proportion to its transfer rate. In the worst case the radar is discovered but no usable picture ever appears.

How much data each radar sends

Volume differs enormously between brands, and the deciding factor is whether the radar compresses its spokes. Navico sends them raw, which is why it is the heaviest thing on a typical radar network. Furuno and Raymarine Quantum compress, and send far less.

Radar Spokes sent per revolution Bytes per spoke Per revolution
Navico BR24 / 3G / 4G / HALO 2048 512, uncompressed ~1 MB
Furuno DRS4D-NXT ~860 ~200–400, compressed ~0.2–0.35 MB
Raymarine Quantum 250 up to 252 samples, compressed ~0.06 MB
Furuno DRS4W ~630 ~16–24, compressed ~0.01 MB

These are the radars we have wire captures for; the others are not listed rather than guessed at. The Quantum's frugality is not an accident — its picture crosses a WiFi link inside the radar before it reaches anything you plugged in, which is a story of its own: Quantum networking explained. Rotation rate multiplies all of it — a HALO at 48 rpm puts its megabyte on the network twice as often as a radar turning at 24 rpm, which is how a Navico installation reaches the 1 MB/s quoted above.

Note what the bottom of that table implies: the Furuno DRS4W is a WiFi radar, and it gets there by sending about a hundredth of what a Navico sends. A radar designed for WiFi has to be built that way from the start. Putting a radar that was not on a WiFi link does not scale it down — it just loses the difference. We leave it to you to consider what this does to the sharpness and quality of the picture.

The right topology

Run Mayara on a machine with a wired connection to the radar network — a Raspberry Pi on the radar switch is the classic setup. Then reach the Mayara web interface from phones, tablets and laptops over WiFi. Mayara serves the processed picture as ordinary unicast HTTP/WebSocket, which uses the full negotiated WiFi rate and behaves perfectly.

Some access points let you raise the minimum multicast rate above the standard base rate, and with a high enough setting radar data may technically fit. Even then the multicast traffic consumes a large share of the airtime and slows down every other WiFi user on that access point. Wiring the radar path avoids the whole problem.

Mayara skips wireless interfaces entirely unless you start it with --allow-wifi. That flag concerns Mayara's own network interfaces; it exists for the handful of radars that are wireless by design (Furuno DRS4W, Raymarine Quantum). It never connects Mayara to a radar and never asks for credentials.

Checks that apply to every brand

  • Same segment. Mayara and the radar must be on the same Ethernet network — the same subnet, not separated by a routed hop. Sharing the LAN ports of a WiFi router counts as the same network; sitting on different subnets or VLANs does not.
  • Addressing. Some brands need Mayara's interface to sit in a specific subnet, others need a DHCP server on the network for the radar. Check the brand page.
  • IGMP snooping. Managed switches may filter multicast they do not think anyone wants. An unmanaged switch never has this problem; on a managed one, either disable IGMP snooping or make sure a querier is active.
  • Firewall. The host running Mayara must accept inbound UDP on the ports listed on the brand page.
  • Interface selection. On a machine with several networks, you can help Mayara by just using the right one with mayara-server -i <device>. This prevents Mayara from sending (pointless) query requests on the other ethernet devices.
  • Look at the traffic. mayara-server -vv logs discovery packets as they arrive, which immediately separates "the radar is silent" from "the radar is talking and we are not listening".
